How AI is reshaping workflows and redefining jobs

TL;DR

  • A news write-up of the research paper Chaining Tasks, Redefining Work: A Theory of AI Automation by Peyman Shahidi, Mert Demirer and John Horton (MIT Sloan), Nicole Immorlica (Yale / Microsoft) and Brendan Lucier (Microsoft).
  • Its main claim: AI creates most of its value at the level of whole workflows, through how tasks are sequenced, grouped and handed off between humans and machines, not at the level of single tasks.
  • Task chaining: AI-suited tasks that sit next to each other can be run by AI as one sequence; one step that is hard for AI breaks the chain.
  • Handing AI a whole chain can pay off even when humans would do some steps better, because every AI-to-human handoff costs review time.
  • Adopting AI is therefore an organisational design problem. Gains arrive only after a threshold of workflow adaptation and capability is reached.

Key claims

  • AI's biggest impact comes from how it reshapes entire workflows: how tasks are sequenced, grouped and handed off between humans and machines. (lead paragraph; section "AI value emerges at the workflow level")
  • The researchers modelled work as sequences of interdependent tasks. Two roles with similar tasks can get very different value from AI depending on how those tasks are arranged. Lecture-based teaching (content prepared in advance) automates more easily than tutoring (continuous back-and-forth with the student). ("AI value emerges at the workflow level")
  • In task chaining, organisations link several tasks so AI executes them as a continuous sequence. Adjacent AI-friendly tasks can be bundled, and a single hard step can undermine the whole chain. ("AI value emerges at the workflow level")
  • Stated as a design principle: how tasks are clustered matters as much as which tasks are automated. ("AI value emerges at the workflow level")
  • AI does not need to outperform humans at every task to create value. Assigning a whole chain to AI can be worth it even if humans could do some steps better, because each AI-to-human handoff requires review, validation and adjustment. ("Why system-level efficiency beats task-level perfection")
  • Leaders should judge AI on whether it improves the efficiency of the entire workflow, not on whether it excels at each step. ("Why system-level efficiency beats task-level perfection")
  • Jobs have historically been bundles of tasks most efficient for a human. AI lowers the cost of some activities and enables new combinations, so employees may take on more judgement-based work and the distribution of work across teams can be redrawn. ("Redesigning work and expectations for AI")
  • Gains often emerge only after organisations have adapted workflows and built capability. Before that threshold, adoption costs dominate. ("Redesigning work and expectations for AI")
  • Treating AI as a plug-in tool yields incremental improvement. Redesigning workflows (grouping AI-compatible tasks, reducing handoffs) is more likely to unlock its full potential. ("Redesigning work and expectations for AI")

Caveats

  • Secondary source. This is an MIT Sloan news article about a paper by MIT Sloan researchers, published by the school itself. It reports the paper's conclusions but gives no data, model details or method. The paper is linked from the article: https://demirermert.github.io/Papers/Demirer_chaining_tasks_ai_automation.pdf (not yet in raw/).
  • One voice. All quotes come from one co-author (Shahidi). No outside researcher is quoted.
  • Theory, not field evidence. The paper is described as a theory of AI automation. The teaching/tutoring contrast is an illustration, not a measured result.
  • Affiliations. Two co-authors work at Microsoft, which sells AI products.
