# AI vendor dependency

The risk that a firm comes to rely on an AI tool whose owner can change its price, availability, terms or data handling, or can be forced to by government policy. The sources' answer is to know each tool's owner, keep exit options, and use open models where it matters.

## What the sources say

- Most AI tools an SME uses are likely US-owned, so US policy can change their price, availability or terms. [[2026-10-12-aibs-handbook-geopolitics]]
- Closed cloud AI inside existing software is low-effort but carries the highest dependency on its owner. European GenAI4EU-backed solutions and open-weight models on your own PC reduce it. [[2026-10-12-aibs-handbook-geopolitics]]
- Open-weight models let businesses avoid dependence on a closed model provider and keep sensitive data in-house. The chapter notes that even the US AI Action Plan says this, and calls open models the SME's main lever. [[2026-10-12-aibs-handbook-geopolitics]]
- Steps: list every AI tool with its owner, home country and data location; check contracts for data use, price changes and exit terms; define a plan B per tool (a European or open-source option, or a manual process). [[2026-10-12-aibs-handbook-geopolitics]]
- A local open model (LM Studio, Ollama) is feasible on one PC with 16 GB memory and a graphics card, at the cost of weaker models and upkeep. [[2026-09-24-aibs-handbook-trust-ai-safety]]
- An example of the closed, built-in kind: Siemens' Engineering Copilot TIA is delivered as a managed service inside its existing TIA Portal software. [[2025-12-10-siemens-engineering-copilot-tia]] The article itself does not discuss dependency. Placing the copilot in the highest-dependency category is this wiki's reading of the geopolitics chapter's classification. [[2026-10-12-aibs-handbook-geopolitics]]

Confidence note: two sources are chapters of the same course handbook, and the third only supplies an example, so 0.75 is the ceiling.
