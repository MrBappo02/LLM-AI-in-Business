# AI regulation

Rules that govern how AI systems are developed, tested and released, and what happens when they cause harm. The central question is who sets the rules: binding law, or the industry regulating itself.

## What the sources say

- Microsoft's president argues AI is less regulated than everyday products: washing machines and milk face safety rules and inspection, while AI does not. He says existing product-safety practice should be applied to AI. [[2026-10-11-ai-is-less-regulated-than-milk]]
- His proposed measures:
  - emergency brakes (kill switches) to pause or slow AI that gets out of control;
  - a legal requirement to write incident reports;
  - a legal duty to tell victims when a rogue AI agent breaches their systems;
  - independent government evaluators who sign off on AI products before release.

  [[2026-10-11-ai-is-less-regulated-than-milk]]
- **Self-regulation vs. binding rules.** The Trump administration says the sector should regulate itself so US firms don't lose to China. Its main instrument is a "morally binding" pledge, signed by leading AI firms, to work with independent auditors. Microsoft's president calls this pledge necessary but no longer sufficient: self-regulation binds only the responsible companies. [[2026-10-11-ai-is-less-regulated-than-milk]]
- **Who leads.** The same source says the UK is best placed to lead global rules, because of its AI Security Institute (formerly the AI Safety Institute). It also says Washington would accept UK standards more readily than EU ones. [[2026-10-11-ai-is-less-regulated-than-milk]]

## Caveat on the evidence

The only source so far is a news report of one AI vendor's executive's views. It shows a position in the debate, not evidence that any measure works. Confidence stays at 0.7 until independent sources (policy analysis, legal texts such as the EU AI Act, or evaluations of regulatory regimes) arrive.
