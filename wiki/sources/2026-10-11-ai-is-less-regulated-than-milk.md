# AI is less regulated than milk, says Microsoft president

## TL;DR

- BBC business news report on an interview with Microsoft president Brad Smith on the *Big Boss Interview* podcast.
- Smith says milk and washing machines face more safety rules than AI. He calls for **global AI safety rules**, starting now.
- His proposals:
  - emergency brakes (kill switches);
  - legally required incident reports;
  - a duty to tell victims when a rogue AI agent breaches their systems;
  - independent government evaluators who sign off before release.
- He backs the White House's "morally binding" industry pledge but calls self-regulation no longer sufficient, because it only binds the responsible companies.
- This puts him at odds with President Trump, who wants the sector to regulate itself so US firms don't lose to China. Smith says the UK, through its AI Security Institute, is best placed to lead.

## Key claims

- Smith says AI is subject to fewer safety rules than washing machines or milk, which face safety rules and inspection. (opening paragraphs)
- He calls for global safety rules. These include a legal obligation to tell a victim if their system is breached by a rogue AI agent, and deciding when outside evaluators should sign off on an AI product before release. (paragraph 2)
- Trump has said the sector should regulate itself so US firms don't lose out to Chinese rivals. (paragraph 3)
- The article says demands for AI rules grew after experts in the sector said they believe the technology is a threat to humanity. It also says the bosses of Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind backed the idea of an AI development slowdown, which Trump has pushed against. (paragraphs 5–6, the reporter's framing)
- Smith says firms need emergency brakes (kill switches) to pause or slow AI that gets out of control. He also wants a legal requirement to write incident reports and tell victims, and independent government evaluators to vet new AI technology. His argument is that society already knows how to do this for other products. (middle section)
- Smith says the UK is best placed to drive global AI rules because of its AI Security Institute, launched under Rishi Sunak as the AI Safety Institute. (middle section)
- Smith suggests Washington would accept UK-made standards more readily than EU ones, because people there do not look at London the way they look at Brussels. (section "'The moment has arrived'")
- He cites AI agents that have escaped human control, and models so powerful they put the world's cybersecurity at risk, as reasons to act now. (same section)
- At a White House meeting at the end of the previous month, executives of OpenAI, Anthropic, Nvidia, SpaceX, Meta and Google signed a "morally binding" document. In it they agreed to work with independent auditors to check that their AI systems work as intended. Smith applauds it and says Microsoft is happy to sign, but calls it no longer sufficient. (closing section)

## Quotes

> Right now, your washing machine is subject to more safety rules than AI.

> We know how to do this for other products

> It's a good step, it's a necessary step... But I don't think it's sufficient any longer.

> It's the companies that are not as responsible that you need to worry about.

## Caveats

- **One executive's views**, from a podcast interview, reported by a journalist. This is not evidence that the proposed measures work or are feasible.
- **Interested party.** Microsoft builds and sells AI, so it has a stake in the rules it proposes. The article does not discuss this.
- The article does not say which incidents of "AI agents that have escaped human control" Smith means.
